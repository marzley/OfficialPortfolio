# B. Transcription and audio work (ways 13–21): transcription, captions, voice-over and podcast editing

Every day, millions of hours of audio and video are recorded: interviews, meetings, lectures, court sessions, podcasts, YouTube videos, doctor's notes and customer calls. Much of it needs to become **text** (transcripts and captions), or needs a **human voice** (voice-overs, narration). This unit covers nine audio-related ways to earn, from beginner-friendly transcription to skilled voice work.

:::note What all audio work needs
- **Good headphones** (over-ear or quality earphones): you can't transcribe accurately from phone speakers.
- **A quiet place** and patience.
- **Fast, accurate typing** (aim for 50+ words per minute with high accuracy; free practice on typing.com, monkeytype.com, 10fastfingers.com).
- **Excellent listening and English spelling**, including names, places and technical terms (research them).
- A laptop (most transcription is impractical on a phone).
:::

---

### 13. General transcription

**What it is.** Listening to audio or video and typing exactly what is said, following the platform's **style guide** (how to mark speakers, unclear words, timestamps, filler words).

**Two styles you'll meet:**
- **Clean verbatim:** remove "um", "uh", false starts and repetitions; fix stutters. The most common.
- **Full (true) verbatim:** type everything exactly, including "um", laughter [laughs], pauses and false starts. Used in research and legal work.

**How to start, step by step:**
1. **Practise first:** transcribe 5–10 minutes of a podcast or YouTube interview and compare with its captions. Note your mistakes.
2. **Learn the tools:** a free transcription player like **oTranscribe** (otranscribe.com) lets you pause, rewind and slow audio with keyboard shortcuts while typing.
3. **Read the style guide carefully** before any test. Most failures are style guide mistakes, not hearing mistakes.
4. **Apply to platforms** (each has an entry test; availability for Kenyan applicants changes, so check the current application page):
   - **GoTranscript** (gotranscript.com): popular with Kenyan transcribers; strict tests.
   - **TranscribeMe** (transcribeme.com): short audio clips; pathways to specialised teams.
   - **Rev** (rev.com/freelancers): transcription and captions.
   - **Scribie** (scribie.com): short files, a good place to practise.
   - **Upwork/Fiverr**: direct clients (researchers, podcasters, journalists).
5. **Start with short, clear files**, build your accuracy score, then take longer and harder files (accents, poor audio) that pay more per minute.
6. **Find direct clients:** university researchers (interview transcripts), journalists, NGOs (focus group discussions), churches (sermon transcripts), YouTubers.

**Pay (honest).** Platforms usually pay **per audio minute**. Beginners often find it slow at first: one hour of audio can take 3–5 hours to transcribe until you get faster. Income improves with speed, accuracy, specialisation and direct clients. Work availability on platforms goes up and down.

:::warning Transcription scams
"Pay KSh 1,500 to activate your transcription account" or "buy a transcription account" are scams. Real platforms are free to join. Buying or renting accounts breaks rules and can get you banned without payment.
:::

### 14. Captioning and subtitling

**What it is.** Creating **captions** (same-language text synchronised with video, including sound cues like [music]) and **subtitles** (translations) for YouTube videos, films, online courses, TV and events.

**How to start:**
1. Learn caption rules: maximum characters per line (often around 32–42), 1–2 lines, reading speed, timing to speech, speaker identification.
2. Learn free tools: **Subtitle Edit** (Windows, free), **Aegisub**, or YouTube Studio's subtitle editor. Understand formats: **SRT** and **WebVTT**.
3. Make samples: caption a 3-minute public-domain or your own video in SRT.
4. Apply to captioning platforms (Rev captions, Verbit, 3Play Media, subtitle agencies) and offer YouTubers captioning; many Kenyan creators and churches want Kiswahili or English captions.
5. Offer **subtitle translation** (English ↔ Kiswahili) for films and NGO videos.

**Why it pays well.** Captions require timing skills as well as transcription; accessibility laws in many countries require captions on public videos, so demand is steady.

### 15. Specialist transcription (medical and legal)

**What it is.** Transcribing doctors' dictations, medical reports (medical transcription), court proceedings, depositions and legal interviews (legal transcription).

**Who it suits.** People with medical or legal training (nurses, clinical officers, paralegals, law graduates) or anyone willing to study the terminology seriously.

**How to start:**
1. Learn the terminology (medical: anatomy, drugs, abbreviations; legal: court procedures, Latin terms).
2. Take a reputable course if needed; some employers require certification.
3. Understand **confidentiality**: medical and legal records are highly sensitive; follow data protection rules strictly.
4. Apply to specialist teams on transcription platforms, or medical transcription/BPO companies (some operate in Kenya, serving international hospitals).

**Pay.** Higher than general transcription because errors can be dangerous (a wrong drug dose) and expertise is scarce.

**Note.** AI speech recognition now does much of the first draft in medicine, so many jobs are now **editing AI transcripts** for accuracy. The skill of checking carefully remains valuable.

### 16. Kiswahili and local-language transcription

**What it is.** Transcribing and translating audio in Kiswahili, Sheng, Kikuyu, Dholuo, Luhya, Kalenjin, Kamba, Somali and other languages, for researchers, NGOs, media monitoring, market research and AI companies.

**Why it's an opportunity.** Fewer people can do it, and there is far less automatic transcription for African languages, so human transcribers are needed.

**How to start:**
1. Practise transcribing local radio interviews or vernacular sermons, then translating them to English.
2. Contact research firms, universities and NGOs that run surveys and focus groups (they need transcripts and translations).
3. Watch for language projects on AI data platforms (method 32) and translation agencies.
4. Keep your spelling consistent (agree on a spelling standard with the client).

### 17. Voice-over work

**What it is.** Recording your voice for adverts, explainer videos, e-learning courses, YouTube videos, phone systems (IVR: "Press 1 for..."), radio and TV, animations and audiobooks.

**Who it suits.** Clear, pleasant voices; people who can read naturally and take direction. Kenyan English, Kiswahili and African accents are increasingly requested for local and pan-African campaigns.

**How to start, step by step:**
1. **Set up a home studio** cheaply: a quiet room with soft furnishings (curtains, a mattress, blankets reduce echo; many beginners record in a wardrobe full of clothes), a USB microphone (e.g. entry-level condenser mics), a pop filter, and free **Audacity** software.
2. **Learn basic audio editing** in Audacity: noise reduction, cutting breaths and mistakes, normalising volume, exporting MP3/WAV.
3. **Record a demo reel**: 60–90 seconds with short clips in different styles (commercial, corporate, e-learning, character, Kiswahili).
4. **Create profiles** on **Fiverr**, **Voices.com**, **Voice123** and **Upwork**; send your demo to Kenyan production houses, radio stations, ad agencies and e-learning companies.
5. **Deliver professionally:** clean audio, on time, with one free revision for mistakes.

**Pay.** Varies hugely: short Fiverr gigs pay little; commercial ads (especially TV/radio) pay well and sometimes include usage fees.

### 18. Audiobook narration

**What it is.** Reading whole books aloud for audiobook platforms.

**How to start:**
1. Build voice-over basics (method 17) and stamina (narrating one finished hour typically takes several hours of recording and editing).
2. Learn audiobook standards (consistent volume, room tone, chapter files, noise floor).
3. Find work: **ACX** (Audible's marketplace) is limited to certain countries (mainly US, UK, Canada and Ireland residents), so from Kenya look at Upwork/Fiverr narration jobs, publishers of African literature, and authors self-publishing directly.
4. Record a sample chapter of a public-domain book (e.g. from Project Gutenberg) for your portfolio.

### 19. Podcast editing

**What it is.** Editing podcast recordings: removing mistakes and long pauses, balancing volume between speakers, adding intro music, cleaning noise, creating show notes and short clips.

**How to start:**
1. Learn **Audacity** (free) or **Reaper** (affordable) or **Descript** (edits audio by editing the transcript).
2. Practise on free raw podcast files or record your own conversation.
3. Offer packages: "Edit + show notes + 3 short clips for social media".
4. Find clients: Kenyan podcasters (a growing scene), churches, companies starting podcasts, and international podcasters on Upwork/Fiverr.
5. Add video podcast editing (most podcasts now publish on YouTube) to earn more.

---

### 20. Voice data recording for AI

**What it is.** Recording yourself reading sentences or having conversations to train speech recognition and voice AI, often for under-served languages and accents (Kenyan English, Kiswahili, local languages).

**How to start:**
1. Look for **speech data projects** on AI data platforms (Appen, OneForma, and others) and on research project announcements from universities.
2. Follow instructions exactly (quiet room, specific microphone distance, reading exactly as written).
3. Understand that these are usually **short projects**, not continuous work.

**Watch out.** Read consent terms: you're giving permission to use your voice to train AI. Never pay to join a "voice project". Note that some valuable language projects (like Mozilla Common Voice) are **volunteer** and unpaid; contributing is good for your language, but it isn't income.

---

### 21. Audio cleanup and music services

**What it is.** Cleaning noisy recordings (interviews, church services, wedding videos), mixing and mastering songs for local artists, producing jingles and radio adverts, making beats.

**How to start:**
1. Learn a DAW (digital audio workstation): **Audacity** for cleanup; **Reaper**, **FL Studio** or **Cakewalk** (free on Windows) for music.
2. Learn mixing basics: EQ, compression, noise reduction, loudness standards for streaming.
3. Build a portfolio: before/after cleanup samples; jingles for imaginary businesses.
4. Clients: local musicians, churches, radio stations, small businesses needing jingles; online, beat-selling on platforms like BeatStars and services on Fiverr.

**Copyright.** Only use samples and loops you have the rights to; register your own music with Kenya's collective management organisations if you release songs.

## A typical first month in transcription

| Week | Focus |
|---|---|
| 1 | Typing practice daily; learn oTranscribe; transcribe 3 practice files and compare with captions |
| 2 | Study two platforms' style guides; take their tests |
| 3 | Start with short files; track accuracy feedback |
| 4 | Contact 10 local researchers, NGOs or podcasters with an offer |

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Fix |
|---|---|
| Ignoring the style guide | Read it twice; keep it open while working |
| Guessing unclear words | Use the platform's [inaudible] tag correctly, research names |
| Phone speakers instead of headphones | Use headphones |
| Echoey voice recordings | Record in a room with soft furnishings |
| Paying to "activate" an account | Real platforms are free; it's a scam |
| Sharing confidential audio | Follow confidentiality and data protection rules |

## Practice tasks

1. Take a typing test and record your words per minute and accuracy; practise 15 minutes daily for two weeks.
2. Transcribe 5 minutes of a podcast in clean verbatim, then the same in full verbatim.
3. Make an SRT caption file for a 2-minute video.
4. Record a 60-second voice demo in Audacity with noise reduction applied.
5. Write an email offering transcription and translation to a university research team.

## Summary

- Audio work: general, specialist and local-language transcription; captioning/subtitling; voice-over; narration; podcast editing; voice data for AI; audio cleanup and music.
- You need headphones, a quiet space, fast accurate typing (or a good voice and a simple home studio), and attention to style guides.
- Platforms include GoTranscript, TranscribeMe, Rev, Scribie, Voices.com, Voice123, Upwork and Fiverr; local researchers, NGOs, churches and podcasters are strong direct clients.
- Kiswahili and local-language skills are a real advantage.
- Never pay to join; protect confidential recordings.
