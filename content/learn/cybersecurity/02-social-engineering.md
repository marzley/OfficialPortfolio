# Social engineering, SIM swap and mobile money fraud

Most successful attacks don't break technology; they trick **people**. This is **social engineering**. In Kenya it's the main way mobile money and bank accounts are emptied. Knowing the tricks is the best protection for you, your family and any business you support.

## The psychology attackers use

| Trigger | Sounds like |
|---|---|
| **Urgency** | "Your account will be blocked in 30 minutes!" |
| **Fear** | "This is the police. Your number is linked to a crime." |
| **Authority** | "I'm calling from Safaricom / KRA / your bank." |
| **Greed** | "You've won KSh 250,000 in a promotion." |
| **Helpfulness** | "I sent money to your number by mistake, please send it back." |
| **Trust** | A message that appears to come from a friend's hacked WhatsApp |

## Common scams in Kenya

### "Wrong number" reversal
You get a real-looking SMS saying someone sent you KSh 3,500, then a call asking you to send it back. The SMS is **fake**: they send it from a normal number. **Always check your actual M-Pesa balance** (or statement) before doing anything. Genuine M-Pesa messages come from "MPESA", not a phone number. Real mistaken transactions are reversed through Safaricom (the sender forwards their M-Pesa message to 456), so you never need to send anything back yourself.

### Fake customer care
"Hello, this is Safaricom customer care. We're upgrading your line; please tell us the code we just sent you." **Safaricom, banks and KRA never ask for your PIN, password or one-time code.** The only official customer care number is 100 / 200 (Safaricom) or the number on the back of your bank card.

### SIM swap
An attacker convinces or bribes someone to move your number to a new SIM card. Your phone suddenly shows **"No service"**, and meanwhile they receive your M-Pesa and bank codes.

Protect yourself:

- If your line unexpectedly loses service in an area with good network, **act immediately**: call your provider from another phone and your bank.
- Don't post your ID number, date of birth or mother's name publicly (they're used to pass verification).
- Use app-based 2FA or bank apps with device binding where possible, not only SMS.

### Job and loan scams
"Pay a registration fee of KSh 1,500 to secure the job / release the loan." Genuine employers and licensed lenders don't ask you to pay to be hired or to receive a loan.

### WhatsApp account takeover
"Hi, I sent you a 6-digit code by mistake, please forward it." That code is **your** WhatsApp registration code. Sharing it gives them your account, which they use to scam your contacts. Turn on **two-step verification** in WhatsApp Settings → Account.

### Business email and invoice fraud
A supplier's email is hacked, and you receive "our bank details have changed" with a new account. **Always confirm payment detail changes by phone, using a number you already had.**

## Other social engineering techniques

| Technique | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Vishing** | Voice calls pretending to be a bank, police, etc. |
| **Smishing** | SMS phishing with links |
| **Pretexting** | A made-up story to get information ("I'm from IT, I need your password to fix your email") |
| **Baiting** | A "free" USB drive or free software containing malware |
| **Tailgating** | Following an employee through a secure door |
| **Shoulder surfing** | Watching you type your PIN |

## The golden rules

1. **Never share** your PIN, passwords or one-time codes. Not with anyone.
2. **Slow down.** Urgency is a red flag. Hang up and call back on the **official** number.
3. **Verify independently**: check balances yourself; confirm changes by a known phone number.
4. **Too good to be true** usually is.
5. **Report** it: forward scam SMS to **333** (Safaricom), report to your bank, and report cybercrime to the DCI.

## For businesses

- Train staff with real examples, regularly.
- Require two people to approve payments to new accounts.
- Have a clear rule: IT will never ask for your password.
- Make reporting easy and never punish people for reporting a mistake quickly.

## Why people are the main target

Most successful attacks start by tricking a person rather than breaking technology: a convincing call, a fake email from the "CEO", a WhatsApp message from a "friend" with a new number, or a USB drive left in a car park. Banks, SACCOs, schools, hospitals and businesses of every size lose money to social engineering. Training people to recognise these tricks is one of the most effective and cheapest security measures there is.

## Phishing emails: anatomy of a fake

| Clue | Example |
|---|---|
| Sender address doesn't match the company | `support@kcb-secure-verify.com` instead of the bank's real domain |
| Generic greeting | "Dear customer" |
| Urgency or threat | "Your account will be closed in 24 hours" |
| Link text differs from the real destination | Hover shows `http://bit.ly/...` or an odd domain |
| Unexpected attachment | `Invoice_2026.zip`, `Payment.html`, macro-enabled documents |
| Requests for credentials or payment | "Confirm your PIN", "Pay this invoice to our new account" |
| Spelling and design mistakes | Though many phishing emails now look professional, especially with AI tools |

Before clicking, **hover** over links (or long-press on a phone) to see the real address. When in doubt, go to the website or app directly instead of using the link.

## Business email compromise (BEC)

A criminal gains access to (or imitates) an email account of a supplier, lawyer or executive, then sends a believable request:

> "Hi, we've changed banks. Please pay invoice #4471 to our new account at..."

Defences:

- **Verify payment-detail changes by phone**, using a number you already have (not one in the email).
- Require two people to approve large or unusual payments.
- Turn on multi-factor authentication (MFA) on all email accounts.
- Watch for lookalike domains (`rn` instead of `m`, `.co` instead of `.co.ke`).

## Voice and video impersonation

AI tools can imitate voices and even faces in video calls. A "director" calling urgently for a transfer, or a "relative" in trouble asking for money, may be fake.

- Agree on a **verification question or code word** with family and finance teams.
- Hang up and call back on a known number.
- Never let urgency push you past normal checks.

## WhatsApp and social media takeovers

Common trick: "I sent you a code by mistake, please forward it to me." That code is the verification code for **your** WhatsApp account; sharing it gives the criminal your account, which they use to scam your contacts.

Protect yourself:

- Turn on **two-step verification** in WhatsApp (Settings → Account → Two-step verification) with a PIN.
- Never share verification codes with anyone.
- If a friend asks for money from a new number or with an unusual story, call them on their known number first.

## Physical social engineering

| Technique | Example | Defence |
|---|---|---|
| Tailgating | Someone follows staff through a secure door | Don't hold secure doors for strangers; ask for ID |
| Pretexting | "I'm from IT, I need your password to fix your email" | IT never needs your password |
| Baiting | Infected USB drive labelled "Salaries 2026" | Never plug in unknown USB devices |
| Shoulder surfing | Watching you type your PIN | Cover the keypad; privacy screens |
| Dumpster diving | Reading thrown-away documents | Shred documents with personal data |

## A security awareness programme for a small business

1. **Short training** for all staff (30 to 60 minutes) on phishing, M-Pesa and payment scams, passwords and MFA.
2. **Clear rules**: how to verify payment changes, who to call about suspicious messages.
3. **Easy reporting**: a WhatsApp group or email address for "Is this a scam?" questions, with no blame for asking.
4. **Simulated phishing** tests (with management approval) to practise.
5. **Regular reminders** through short monthly tips.
6. **Celebrate reporting**: someone who reports a suspicious email may have prevented a major loss.

## What to do if you clicked or shared something

| Situation | Immediate action |
|---|---|
| Entered a password on a fake site | Change that password (and anywhere it's reused), turn on MFA, check account activity |
| Shared an M-Pesa or bank PIN/OTP | Call your provider/bank immediately to block transactions; change PIN |
| Opened a suspicious attachment | Disconnect from the network, tell IT, run a malware scan |
| Sent money to a scammer | Contact your bank or mobile money provider immediately and report to the police |
| WhatsApp hijacked | Re-register WhatsApp with your number to log the attacker out, enable two-step verification, warn contacts |

Speed matters: reporting quickly greatly improves the chance of stopping damage.

## Practice

1. Find a real phishing example (your spam folder, or a published example) and list five warning signs.
2. Turn on two-step verification in WhatsApp and MFA on your email.
3. Write a one-page payment verification policy for a small business.
4. Create a family code word for emergency money requests.
5. Practise hovering over links in five emails and identifying the true destinations.

:::think An accounts clerk receives an email from the director's correct email address asking for an urgent KSh 450,000 payment to a new supplier, "before the end of today, I'm in a meeting, don't call". What should the clerk do?
Treat it as suspicious despite the correct address (the account may be compromised or spoofed): the urgency, new supplier and "don't call" are classic BEC signs. Verify by calling the director on a known number or confirming in person, follow the two-person approval rule for large payments, and report the email to IT.
:::
