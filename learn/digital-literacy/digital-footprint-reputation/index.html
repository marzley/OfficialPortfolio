Your digital footprint, social media and online reputation

Everything you post, like, comment on and search leaves a trail: your digital footprint. Employers, universities, clients and even landlords look people up online. Managing your footprint protects your privacy and opens doors.

Active vs passive footprint

ActivePassive
What you deliberately share: posts, photos, comments, CVs, reviewsWhat's collected about you: location history, searches, cookies, app usage, being tagged

Both can be found, copied and screenshotted, even after you delete them.

Google yourself

Search your name (in quotes, e.g. "Brian Kiprop") with your town or school. Check Google Images too. What would a potential employer think?

Clean up and lock down

  1. Review old posts: delete or archive anything offensive, embarrassing or that shares private details.
  2. Privacy settings: Facebook, Instagram and TikTok → Settings → Privacy. Limit who sees posts, who can tag you, and who can find you by phone number.
  3. Hide personal details: don't publish your phone number, ID number, home location, date of birth or children's school.
  4. Turn off location on photos and posts, and be careful posting while you're away from home ("On holiday for 2 weeks!").
  5. Remove old accounts you no longer use.

Build a positive footprint

Your footprint can also help you:

  • A professional LinkedIn profile: clear photo, headline ("ICT student | Web designer | Nakuru"), skills, projects and certificates.
  • A simple portfolio website or GitHub for developers (see our HTML and Git tutorials).
  • Share useful content about your field: what you're learning, projects you finished, helpful tips.
  • Consistent, professional usernames: amina.designs beats sexy_amina254 for work.

Think before you post: the 5-question test

  1. Would I be happy for my parent, teacher or future boss to see this?
  2. Is it true, and is it kind?
  3. Am I sharing someone else's private information or photo without permission?
  4. Could this be used to find or harm me (location, routine, ID)?
  5. Would I say this to the person's face?

Online reputation and the law

In Kenya, laws such as the Computer Misuse and Cybercrimes Act and the Data Protection Act cover issues like cyberbullying, harassment, sharing intimate images without consent, and publishing false information. Posts can have real legal consequences, not just social ones.

Cyberbullying

If you or someone you know is being bullied online:

  • Don't reply in anger.
  • Keep evidence: screenshots with dates.
  • Block and report the account on the platform.
  • Tell someone you trust: a parent, teacher, counsellor.
  • Serious threats: report to the police.

Digital wellbeing

Social media is designed to keep you scrolling. Use Android's Digital Wellbeing or iPhone Screen Time to see your usage and set app timers, turn off non-essential notifications, and keep phones away at bedtime.

Why your digital footprint matters

Employers, universities, scholarship panels, clients and even landlords increasingly search people online before deciding. Old posts, photos and comments can stay online for years, be screenshotted, and be found by anyone. At the same time, a positive footprint (a professional LinkedIn profile, a portfolio, helpful posts) can win you jobs and clients. Your footprint is also about privacy: companies and criminals collect data about you to target adverts or scams.

Remember: messages "deleted for everyone" may already have been screenshotted, and content sent to groups can be forwarded endlessly.

Audit your footprint in 30 minutes

  1. Search your full name in Google (in quotes: "Jane Wanjiru"), plus your name and town, school or employer. Check the Images tab too.
  2. Look at your profiles while logged out (or ask a friend) to see what the public sees.
  3. Review old posts and photos: delete or archive anything you wouldn't want an employer to see.
  4. Check tagged photos and remove tags if needed.
  5. Review which apps are connected to your Google and Facebook accounts and remove unused ones.
  6. Check privacy settings on every platform: who can see posts, friend lists, phone number and email.
  7. Set up a Google Alert for your name to know when new content appears.

Building a positive professional footprint

  • LinkedIn: a clear photo, headline ("Web Developer | Nakuru | HTML, CSS, JavaScript"), summary, skills, projects and certificates. Connect with classmates, lecturers and professionals.
  • Portfolio: a simple website or GitHub profile showing your work.
  • Share what you learn: short posts about projects and courses show growth.
  • Be helpful: answering questions in professional groups builds reputation.
  • Consistent name and photo across professional platforms makes you easy to find.
  • Recommendations: ask lecturers, supervisors or clients for LinkedIn recommendations.

Kenyan law and your online behaviour

  • The Computer Misuse and Cybercrimes Act covers offences such as cyber harassment, publishing false information intended to cause harm, unauthorised access to computers and online fraud.
  • The Data Protection Act gives you rights over your personal data: to be informed, to access it, to correct it and to request deletion in certain cases. Complaints can go to the Office of the Data Protection Commissioner (ODPC).
  • Defamation (false statements that damage someone's reputation) can lead to court cases, including for social media posts.
  • Sharing intimate images without consent is a serious offence.

This is general information, not legal advice. When in doubt, don't post.

Recognising misinformation

Before sharing a shocking message, photo or video:

  1. Check the source: is it a known, credible news outlet or an anonymous forward?
  2. Search the claim: have fact-checkers (such as PesaCheck or Africa Check) or major media covered it?
  3. Reverse image search: Google Lens or images.google.com shows whether a photo is old or from another place.
  4. Watch for emotional manipulation: content designed to make you angry or afraid spreads fastest.
  5. Check the date: old stories are often re-shared as new.
  6. AI-generated content: images, voices and videos can be faked; look for odd details and confirm with trusted sources.

"Forwarded many times" is not evidence. If you're not sure, don't share.

Protecting children and teens online

  • Use family settings: Google Family Link, YouTube Kids, app age ratings.
  • Talk openly about what to do if something upsetting happens online: tell a trusted adult.
  • Teach them never to share their location, school name and daily routine publicly.
  • Watch for signs of cyberbullying or grooming: secrecy, mood changes, new "friends" offering gifts.

Practice

  1. Google yourself and write down what an employer would find.
  2. Review privacy settings on two social media accounts and make three improvements.
  3. Create or update a LinkedIn profile with a headline, summary and three skills.
  4. Fact-check a viral message you received recently using a reverse image search or a fact-checking site.
  5. Set up a Google Alert for your name.
Think about it: A student posts angry comments with insults about a company on a public page. Years later, the same company is hiring and the student applies. What could happen, and what's a better way to complain?Show answer

Recruiters may find the posts and reject the application, and insults or false claims could even raise legal issues. A better approach is to complain directly and politely (customer care, email, the official complaints process), state facts calmly, and escalate to regulators or consumer bodies if needed. Calm, factual complaints protect both your rights and your reputation.
