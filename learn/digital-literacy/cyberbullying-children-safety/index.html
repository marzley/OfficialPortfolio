Cyberbullying, harassment and keeping children safe online: a guide for young people and parents

The internet gives young people learning, creativity and friendship, but also exposes them to bullying, harassment, scams, inappropriate content, grooming and sextortion. Adults face harassment too. This unit explains what these harms look like, how to respond, how to support someone affected, and practical steps parents, teachers and guardians can take to keep children safer online.

What you will learn

  • What cyberbullying and online harassment are, with examples
  • How to respond: don't engage, document, block, report, get support
  • Sextortion and image-based abuse: what to do (never pay)
  • Grooming warning signs
  • Parental controls and family rules by age
  • Talking to children about online safety
  • Where to get help in Kenya

Cyberbullying and harassment

Cyberbullying

Using phones, social media, messaging, games or other online platforms to repeatedly hurt, threaten, embarrass or exclude someone.

FormExamples
HarassmentRepeated insulting or threatening messages
HumiliationPosting embarrassing photos, videos or edited images; mocking memes
ExclusionDeliberately leaving someone out of groups, then mocking them
ImpersonationCreating fake accounts in someone's name to post damaging content
Outing and doxxingSharing private information (secrets, address, phone number) without consent
Hate and discriminationAttacks based on ethnicity, religion, gender, disability, appearance
ThreatsThreats of violence, or to leak information

Effects can be serious: anxiety, depression, falling grades, withdrawal from friends and activities, and in some cases self-harm. Cyberbullying can follow a young person everywhere, all day, through their phone.

If you're being bullied or harassed

  1. Don't respond or retaliate. Bullies want a reaction; responding can escalate it.
  2. Document everything: screenshots with dates, usernames and links.
  3. Block and report the accounts on the platform (every major platform has reporting tools for harassment, impersonation and threats).
  4. Tighten privacy settings: private account, limit who can message or tag you.
  5. Tell someone you trust: a parent, teacher, counsellor or friend. You don't have to handle it alone.
  6. Report serious cases: threats of violence, sharing intimate images, persistent harassment or impersonation can be crimes (Kenya's Computer Misuse and Cybercrimes Act covers cyber harassment). Report to the police/DCI with your evidence.

If you see someone being bullied

  • Don't like, share or join in.
  • Support the person privately ("I saw what happened; are you okay?").
  • Report the content.
  • Tell a trusted adult if it's serious.

Sextortion and image-based abuse

Sextortion is when someone threatens to share intimate images or videos unless the victim pays money or sends more images. It often starts with a fake profile pretending to be an attractive young person, a quick friendship or romance, and pressure to share images or join a video call that is secretly recorded.

If it happens to you or your child:

  1. Don't pay and don't send more. Paying usually leads to more demands.
  2. Stop communicating, but don't delete the evidence: take screenshots of the profile, messages and demands.
  3. Report the account to the platform and to the police/DCI.
  4. Tell a trusted adult. The victim is not to blame; the criminal is.
  5. Some platforms and organisations offer tools to help remove or block intimate images from spreading; report through the platform's dedicated forms.

Sharing intimate images of anyone under 18 is a serious crime, even if they're shared between young people.

Grooming: warning signs

Grooming is when an adult builds a relationship with a child online to exploit them. Warning signs in a child's behaviour or contacts:

  • An "older friend" met online who gives lots of attention, gifts, airtime or money.
  • Requests to keep the friendship secret.
  • Moving conversations to private apps.
  • Asking for photos, personal details or meetings.
  • The child becoming secretive about devices, upset after being online, or having new items they can't explain.

Teach children: real friends don't ask you to keep secrets from your parents, never meet online-only contacts without a parent, and always tell a trusted adult if something makes them uncomfortable.

Keeping children safe: a parent's guide

Family rules by age (suggestions)

AgeApproach
Under 6Use devices together; child-friendly apps (YouTube Kids); short sessions
6–12Supervised use in shared spaces; parental controls; agreed screen time; no social media accounts (most platforms require users to be at least 13)
13–17Gradually more independence; regular conversations; privacy settings set together; agreed rules about strangers, sharing images and night-time phone use

Parental controls and settings

  • Android: Google Family Link (screen time limits, app approvals, location).
  • iPhone/iPad: Screen Time (content restrictions, downtime, app limits).
  • YouTube Kids for younger children; Restricted Mode on YouTube.
  • Google SafeSearch on browsers and devices.
  • Router-level filtering (some home routers and internet providers offer family filters).
  • Game consoles and apps have parental settings (purchases, chat with strangers).

Controls help, but they're not a substitute for conversation and trust: children may use friends' phones or find workarounds.

Practical habits

  • Keep devices in shared spaces, especially for younger children; charge phones outside bedrooms at night.
  • Know which apps and games your child uses; try them yourself.
  • Agree rules about personal information: no sharing full name with school, address, phone number, or location publicly.
  • Teach them to recognise scams and fake "free airtime/data" offers.
  • Model good behaviour: children copy adults' screen habits.

How to talk about it

  • Start early and keep it ongoing, not one big lecture.
  • Ask open questions: "What's the funniest thing you saw online this week?" "Has anything online ever made you uncomfortable?"
  • Stay calm if they tell you something worrying; if they fear losing their phone, they'll stop telling you.
  • Reassure them they can always come to you, even if they made a mistake.
Think about it: A 12-year-old tells their parent that an online gaming "friend" asked them to send a photo and keep it secret. How should the parent respond?Show answer

Stay calm and praise the child for telling them. Don't punish the child. Stop contact with the account, save screenshots of the conversation, report the account to the game platform and, given the request for a photo and secrecy (grooming signs), report it to the police/DCI. Review the game's privacy settings (turn off chat with strangers) and keep talking with the child.

Where to get help in Kenya

  • Childline Kenya: call 116 (free, for children and anyone worried about a child's safety).
  • Police / DCI for cybercrimes, threats, sextortion and child exploitation.
  • Platform reporting tools (Facebook, Instagram, TikTok, WhatsApp, YouTube, Snapchat, games).
  • School counsellors, teachers and trusted community or religious leaders.
  • Mental health support: talk to a counsellor or health professional if bullying is affecting wellbeing.

Summary

  • Cyberbullying includes harassment, humiliation, exclusion, impersonation, doxxing, hate and threats; it can seriously affect mental health.
  • Respond by not engaging, documenting, blocking, reporting, tightening privacy and telling a trusted person; serious cases are crimes.
  • Sextortion: never pay, keep evidence, report, and remember the victim isn't to blame.
  • Know grooming signs: secrecy, gifts, private chats, requests for photos or meetings.
  • Parents: age-appropriate rules, parental controls (Family Link, Screen Time), shared spaces, and calm, ongoing conversations.
  • Help: Childline 116, police/DCI, platform reports, counsellors.
