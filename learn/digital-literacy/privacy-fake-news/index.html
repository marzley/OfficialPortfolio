Privacy and fake news: protecting your personal data and checking what you read and share

Every app, website and social network collects data about you: your phone number, location, contacts, photos, what you watch and buy. Some of it is necessary; some is used for advertising; and when it leaks, criminals use it for scams and identity theft. At the same time, false information spreads quickly on WhatsApp, Facebook, TikTok and X, especially during elections, health scares and emergencies. This unit teaches how to protect your privacy and how to check information before believing or sharing it.

What you will learn

  • What personal data is and who collects it
  • App permissions and privacy settings (phone, WhatsApp, Facebook, Google)
  • Location, photos and what they reveal
  • Your rights under Kenya's Data Protection Act
  • What misinformation, disinformation and deepfakes are
  • How to fact-check text, images and videos
  • Sharing responsibly

Part 1: Privacy

What is personal data?

Personal data

Any information that identifies you or can be linked to you: name, ID number, phone number, email, photos, location, KRA PIN, M-Pesa transactions, health records, school records, and even your device ID or browsing habits.

Sensitive personal data needs extra protection: health information, biometrics (fingerprints, face), religion, ethnicity, sexual life, children's data, and financial details.

Who collects your data, and why?

CollectorDataPurpose
Social media (Facebook, TikTok, Instagram)Posts, likes, contacts, location, viewing timePersonalised feeds and targeted ads
Google and phone makersSearches, location history, app useServices and advertising
Mobile money and digital lendersTransactions, sometimes contacts and SMS (if permitted)Payments, credit decisions
Shops, supermarkets, loyalty programmesPurchases, phone numbersMarketing
Government (eCitizen, KRA, SHA)Identity, tax, health registrationServices
Schools, employers, hospitalsRecordsAdministration

Data isn't bad in itself; the problems are over-collection, misuse (spam, harassment, unauthorised sharing) and leaks.

App permissions

Apps ask for permissions: camera, microphone, location, contacts, SMS, files. Give only what's needed.

AppReasonable permissionSuspicious permission
FlashlightNone (or camera flash)Contacts, location, SMS
CalculatorNoneContacts, microphone
MapsLocationSMS
Loan appIdentity verificationYour full contact list (some predatory lenders used contacts to shame borrowers; Kenyan regulators have acted against such practices)

Check permissions: Android: Settings → Apps → [app] → Permissions, or Settings → Privacy → Permission manager. iPhone: Settings → Privacy & Security. Choose "Only while using the app" for location, and remove permissions apps don't need. Delete apps you don't use.

Privacy settings to review

  • WhatsApp: Settings → Privacy: who can see your profile photo, about, last seen and status; who can add you to groups ("My contacts"); turn on two-step verification.
  • Facebook/Instagram/TikTok: make accounts private if you prefer; limit who can see posts, tag you, or find you by phone number; review apps connected to your account.
  • Google: myaccount.google.com → Data & privacy: location history (Timeline), web & app activity, ad personalisation; run the Privacy Checkup.
  • Phone: lock screen notifications (hide message content), app permissions, Find My Device.

Location and photos

  • Photos can contain location data (GPS coordinates) in their metadata. Many social platforms remove it when you upload, but files sent as documents or by email may keep it. Turn off location tagging in your camera if you prefer.
  • Posting "We're away in Mombasa for two weeks!" tells people your house is empty. Share holiday photos after you return.
  • Children's photos: avoid showing school names, uniforms with badges, home addresses or daily routines publicly.

Protecting your data day to day

  • Share your ID number, ID photos and KRA PIN only when necessary and with legitimate organisations.
  • Be careful with "fun" quizzes and forms asking for your mother's maiden name, first school or pet's name (common security questions).
  • Shred or tear documents with personal details before throwing them away.
  • Use strong passwords and 2FA (see the passwords lesson).

Your rights: Kenya's Data Protection Act, 2019

The Act gives you rights over your personal data, including (in summary):

  • To be informed how your data will be used.
  • To access data an organisation holds about you.
  • To object to processing (for example, direct marketing).
  • To have false or misleading data corrected or deleted.
  • To have your data protected and used only for the purposes stated.

The Office of the Data Protection Commissioner (ODPC) oversees the Act; you can file complaints there (for example about spam marketing, data leaks, or unauthorised sharing of your information).

Think about it: An app you downloaded to edit photos asks for access to your contacts and SMS messages. What should you do?Show answer

Deny those permissions. A photo editor has no reason to read your contacts or SMS. If the app refuses to work without them, uninstall it and find a trustworthy alternative. Excessive permissions can mean data harvesting, spam, or worse.

Part 2: Fake news

Types of false information

TypeMeaningExample
MisinformationFalse information shared without intent to harm (the sharer believes it)A relative forwards a fake health cure
DisinformationFalse information spread deliberately to deceiveFake news created to influence an election or damage a rival
MalinformationReal information used to harmLeaking someone's private photos or address
Satire/parodyHumour that some people mistake for real newsA comedy site's made-up headline
DeepfakesAI-generated or altered video, audio or images of real people saying/doing things they never didA fake voice note of a politician or a fake video of a celebrity endorsing a scheme

Why fake news spreads

  • It's designed to trigger strong emotions: anger, fear, outrage, excitement.
  • It confirms what people already believe.
  • Forwarding is easy, and messages from friends feel trustworthy.
  • Algorithms promote content that gets lots of engagement.

How to check information (the SIFT method)

  1. Stop. Notice your emotional reaction. Don't share yet.
  2. Investigate the source. Who published it? Is it a known news organisation or official body, or an unknown page or forwarded message with no source?
  3. Find better coverage. Search the claim: are reliable news outlets or official sites reporting it? If something huge happened, many sources will cover it.
  4. Trace claims to the original. Find the original photo, video, quote or document; check its date and context.

Checking images and videos

  • Reverse image search: Google Lens or images.google.com (upload or paste the image) shows where else the picture appeared. Old photos are often reused with false captions ("flood today" using a photo from another country years ago).
  • Look for signs of manipulation: odd shadows, blurred edges, distorted hands or text in AI images, mismatched lip movements in videos.
  • Check the date and location: are the weather, signs, number plates and language consistent with the claim?
  • Deepfake audio: be very careful with voice notes claiming to be from leaders or family members asking for money; verify directly.

Fact-checking resources

  • Kenyan and African fact-checkers such as PesaCheck and Africa Check publish checks on viral claims.
  • Official sources: government ministries and agencies (.go.ke), the Ministry of Health, the IEBC for elections, the Kenya Meteorological Department for weather alerts.
  • Reputable news organisations (compare several).

Sharing responsibly

Before forwarding, ask:

  • Do I know this is true?
  • Is the source reliable and identified?
  • Is it current?
  • Could sharing it harm someone (panic, hatred, embarrassment)?
  • Would I be comfortable if it were proven false with my name attached?

If unsure, don't share. If a group is spreading something false, kindly share a fact-check.

Hate speech and incitement

Sharing content that incites violence or hatred against people based on ethnicity, religion or other characteristics is harmful and can be a criminal offence in Kenya (the National Cohesion and Integration Commission works against hate speech). Publishing false information in some circumstances can also lead to legal consequences.

Practice tasks

  1. Review app permissions on your phone and remove at least three unnecessary ones.
  2. Run Google's Privacy Checkup and adjust WhatsApp privacy settings.
  3. Take a viral claim from a family group and check it with SIFT and a fact-checking site.
  4. Do a reverse image search on a dramatic photo you've seen shared.
  5. Write a short, kind message you could post in a group when someone shares false information.

Summary

  • Personal data identifies you; sensitive data (health, biometrics, finances, children's data) needs extra care.
  • Limit app permissions, review privacy settings (WhatsApp, social media, Google), and think before posting locations and children's details.
  • The Data Protection Act (2019) gives you rights; the ODPC handles complaints.
  • Misinformation (unintentional), disinformation (deliberate), malinformation and deepfakes all spread through emotion and easy sharing.
  • Check before sharing with SIFT, reverse image search, fact-checkers (PesaCheck, Africa Check) and official sources.
  • If unsure, don't share; avoid hate speech.
