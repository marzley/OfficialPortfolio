Freelancing from Kenya: how it really works and your 90-day roadmap

A freelancer sells a skill to clients project by project instead of working for one employer. Kenyan freelancers build websites, write articles, design logos, edit videos, manage social media, do bookkeeping, transcribe audio, run ads, support customers, translate, tutor and much more, for clients in Kenya and abroad.

This subject takes you from "I have a skill" to "I run a small freelance business". It goes deeper than the overview lessons in Make money online, and links to them where they already cover something well.

Honest expectations

  • Freelancing is a business, not a job. Nobody gives you a salary. You find clients, deliver work, chase payments and pay your own taxes.
  • The first 1–3 months are usually slow. Many beginners send dozens of proposals before the first contract. That's normal, not a sign you should quit or pay someone for "guaranteed jobs".
  • Income varies. Some months are good, some are quiet. Successful freelancers plan for that with savings and repeat clients.
  • No legitimate platform or client charges you to get work. "Registration fees", "account activation" and "buy this account" offers are scams.

Is freelancing right for you?

You'll probably enjoy freelancing if…You may struggle if…
You can work without a boss checking on youYou need someone to set your daily tasks
You're comfortable talking to strangers about moneyYou avoid asking for payment
You can handle rejection and keep goingOne "no" ruins your week
You like variety and learningYou want the same routine every day
You can save during good monthsEvery shilling gets spent as it arrives

Many people freelance part-time first, alongside studies or a job, and switch to full-time only when freelance income has been steady for several months. That's the safest route.

The freelance business model in one picture

  1. Skill: something people pay for (see skills that pay).
  2. Offer: a clear service for a specific type of client (niche and offer).
  3. Proof: portfolio samples, case studies, reviews (portfolio with no clients).
  4. Clients: from platforms (Upwork, Fiverr) and directly (finding direct clients).
  5. Delivery: doing great work and communicating well (communication and delivery).
  6. Reviews and referrals: each happy client makes the next one easier.

Your 90-day roadmap

Days 1–30: Offer and proof

  • Week 1: choose your niche and one main offer (lesson 2). Write it in one sentence.
  • Week 2–3: build 3–5 portfolio samples that match that offer exactly (lesson 3).
  • Week 4: set up your profiles: one platform (Upwork or Fiverr), LinkedIn, and a simple portfolio page or Google Drive folder. Decide your starting prices (pricing).

Checkpoint: someone who has never met you can understand what you do, for whom, and see proof, in under a minute.

Days 31–60: First clients

  • Send 5–10 tailored proposals or pitches per working day (platform proposals, local businesses, your network).
  • Track every pitch in a spreadsheet: date, client, channel, reply, result.
  • Do discovery calls properly and send clear quotes (lesson 7).
  • Deliver your first projects brilliantly, even if they're small and low-paid, and ask for a review.

Checkpoint: 1–3 paying clients and at least one review or testimonial.

Days 61–90: Improve and repeat

  • Look at your tracker: which channel and which type of pitch got replies? Do more of that.
  • Turn each finished project into a case study.
  • Offer happy clients ongoing work (retainers) and ask for referrals.
  • Raise prices for new clients once you have 3–5 good reviews.

Checkpoint: a repeatable way of finding clients, and your first repeat or referred client.

Sunday, 2 hReview the tracker, plan the week, update profiles

How long until real income?

It depends on the skill, your level, how many pitches you send and luck. Rough patterns many freelancers see:

  • Month 1–2: small first projects, often underpriced, mostly for reviews.
  • Month 3–6: steadier work if you kept pitching and delivering well; first repeat clients.
  • Month 6–12: prices rise, some retainers, referrals start to arrive.

Some get there faster; many take longer. The people who quit usually stop pitching after a few weeks of silence.

Summary

  • Freelancing is running a small business: skill → offer → proof → clients → delivery → reviews.
  • Start part-time, with one niche, one offer and one platform plus direct outreach.
  • Follow the 90-day plan: proof first, then daily pitching, then improve what works.
  • Never pay to get freelance work.
