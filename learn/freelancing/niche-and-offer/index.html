Choosing your niche and creating an offer clients understand

"I do websites, logos, writing, data entry and social media" sounds flexible, but clients read it as "not really good at any of them". Freelancers who win work quickly usually say something much narrower:

"I build fast WordPress websites with M-Pesa checkout for Kenyan online shops."

That's a niche (who you serve) plus an offer (what they get). This lesson helps you choose both.

What a niche is (and isn't)

A niche is a combination of:

PartExamples
SkillWeb design, copywriting, video editing, bookkeeping, transcription, social media management
Client typeSchools, clinics, real-estate agents, restaurants, SaaS start-ups, YouTubers, NGOs, churches
Problem / resultMore bookings, faster payments, clean books for KRA, consistent social posts, captions for accessibility

You don't need all three on day one; skill + client type is enough to start. You can still accept other work. The niche decides how you describe yourself and where you look for clients.

Narrow is not forever

Picking a niche is a marketing decision, not a life sentence. Many freelancers test a niche for 60–90 days, then adjust based on who actually pays and who they enjoy working with.

Step 1: List your sellable skills

Write every skill you could deliver today at a standard someone would pay for. Be honest; "I watched a video about it" doesn't count yet. Use skills that pay and ways to earn online for ideas.

Step 2: Score niche ideas

Score each idea from 1 (low) to 5 (high):

CriteriaQuestion
DemandDo these clients regularly pay for this? (Search Upwork/Fiverr, LinkedIn posts, Kenyan job boards.)
Ability to payDo they have budgets? (A funded start-up vs a student.)
Your skillCan you deliver excellent work now?
AccessCan you reach these clients? (Do you know any? Where do they hang out?)
InterestWill you enjoy this work for a year?
CompetitionCan you stand out? (A Kenyan angle, a language, an industry you know.)

Example:

IdeaDemandPaySkillAccessInterestComp.Total
Websites for private schools in Central Kenya43454424
Logos for anyone worldwide52323116
Shopify store setup for UK small brands45324321
Captions and subtitles for YouTubers43433320

Pick the highest score as your main niche and the second as a backup.

Step 3: Use your unfair advantages

Kenyan freelancers can stand out with things competitors don't have:

  • Local knowledge: M-Pesa, KRA, eCitizen, CBC/CBET, SACCOs, Kenyan consumer behaviour. Foreign agencies can't easily offer "M-Pesa integration" or "content in Sheng and Kiswahili".
  • Industry background: a nurse who writes health content, an accountant who builds Excel dashboards, a teacher who designs learning materials.
  • Languages: Kiswahili, French, Arabic, or local languages for translation, transcription and voice work.
  • Time zone: East Africa Time overlaps with Europe's working day and the Middle East, useful for support and admin roles.

Step 4: Turn the niche into an offer

A strong offer answers five questions:

  1. Who is it for?
  2. What do they get (deliverables)?
  3. What result does it bring?
  4. How long does it take?
  5. How much does it cost (or "from")?

Productised services

Beginners win faster with a productised service: a fixed package with a fixed scope and price, like a product on a shelf. Clients understand it instantly, and it's easier to deliver well.

VagueProductised
"I do social media.""Social Starter: 12 branded posts a month for your Instagram and Facebook, captions in English and Kiswahili, scheduled for you. KSh 12,000/month."
"I write content.""Blog Pack: 4 SEO blog posts (1,200 words each) for dental clinics, with keyword research and images. Delivered in 10 days."
"I build websites.""School Website in 14 Days: 6 pages, admissions form, news section, Google Maps, hosting setup and staff training."
"I edit videos.""Shorts Pack: 10 vertical clips cut from your long video, with captions and hooks, in 72 hours."

Write your offer as a one-page sheet (Google Doc or Canva): title, who it's for, what's included, what's not included, timeline, price, how to start.

Step 5: Positioning statement

Combine everything into one sentence you'll reuse everywhere (profiles, LinkedIn headline, WhatsApp bio, pitches):

I help [client type] get [result] with [service].

Examples:

  • "I help Kenyan private schools get more admissions enquiries with fast, mobile-friendly websites."
  • "I help e-commerce brands turn product videos into scroll-stopping TikToks and Reels."
  • "I help small businesses keep KRA-ready books with monthly bookkeeping in QuickBooks."

Step 6: Validate before you invest months

Test demand quickly:

  • Search the platforms for your service + client type. Are there jobs or gigs with reviews?
  • Message 10 potential clients (people in your network who fit) and ask about their problem, not to buy: "How do you currently handle X? What's annoying about it?"
  • Offer one or two discounted "founding client" projects in exchange for a detailed testimonial.

If nobody bites after a real effort (dozens of conversations), adjust the client type or the offer, not your whole career.

Common mistakes

  • Choosing only by what pays most without the skill to deliver: bad reviews early are hard to recover from.
  • Too many services on one profile.
  • No price at all: "contact me for pricing" scares away many small clients. Show "from KSh …" at least.
  • Copying another freelancer's offer word for word. Use your own story and advantages.

Summary

  • Niche = skill + client type (+ the result you bring). It shapes your message and where you look.
  • Score ideas on demand, ability to pay, skill, access, interest and competition.
  • Package your service as a clear, productised offer with deliverables, timeline and price.
  • Validate with real conversations before spending months on it.
