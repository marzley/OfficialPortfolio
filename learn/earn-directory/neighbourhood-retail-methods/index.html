Neighbourhood shops and retail

Every estate and village needs shops. Retail is competitive, so location, stock control and customer service decide success.

What these methods share

  • Licences: a county single business permit; some goods need extra licences.
  • Stock control: track what sells; avoid dead stock.
  • M-Pesa: a till number and good records help you grow.

866. Neighbourhood shop (duka) or minimart

What it is: Selling everyday goods (sugar, flour, soap, airtime) to nearby households. How it works: Buy from wholesalers → sell at small margins → restock daily.

SkillsStock management, customer service
Cost & equipmentPremises, shelves, stock
Where to find workEstates, villages, near schools
PaymentDaily sales
Realistic earningsLow to medium
Time to first incomeDays

How to start: Choose a busy spot without strong competition; stock what neighbours ask for. Pros: Daily cash flow. Cons & risks: Thin margins; credit to customers. Scam warning: Limit credit to people you trust. Kenya notes: Wholesalers and distributors now deliver to shops, often with digital ordering.

867. Hardware shop

What it is: Selling building materials, tools, paint and plumbing and electrical items. How it works: Stock popular items → serve builders and homeowners → offer delivery.

SkillsProduct knowledge, stock control
Cost & equipmentHigh: premises, stock, transport
Where to find workGrowing estates, rural centres
PaymentDaily sales
Realistic earningsMedium
Time to first incomeMonths

How to start: Start near construction activity; build relationships with fundis. Pros: Steady demand. Cons & risks: High capital tied in stock. Scam warning: Avoid counterfeit electrical goods. Kenya notes: Fast-growing towns create demand for hardware shops.

868. Cereals and grains shop

What it is: Selling maize, beans, rice, green grams and flour by weight. How it works: Buy in bulk → store well → sell by kilo.

SkillsGrain quality, storage
Cost & equipmentPremises, storage, verified scale
Where to find workEstates, markets
PaymentDaily sales
Realistic earningsLow to medium
Time to first incomeWeeks

How to start: Buy at harvest when prices are low; store properly. Pros: Staple demand. Cons & risks: Price swings; pests. Scam warning: Use a verified weighing scale: cheating customers is illegal. Kenya notes: Cereal shops are common in every Kenyan market.

869. Milk ATM and milk bar

What it is: Selling fresh pasteurised milk from a dispensing machine, plus yoghurt and mala. How it works: Buy from processors or coolers → dispense → sell.

SkillsHygiene, customer service
Cost & equipmentMilk dispenser, Kenya Dairy Board licence, premises
Where to find workEstates, campuses
PaymentDaily sales
Realistic earningsLow to medium
Time to first incomeWeeks to months

How to start: Get the Kenya Dairy Board licence; source from licensed processors. Pros: Daily customers. Cons & risks: Spoilage if cooling fails. Scam warning: Never sell raw unprocessed milk from unlicensed sources. Kenya notes: Milk ATMs are popular in Kenyan towns.

870. Stationery and bookshop

What it is: Selling exercise books, textbooks, pens and school supplies. How it works: Stock for school calendars → sell to parents and schools.

SkillsStock planning, school calendar knowledge
Cost & equipmentPremises, stock
Where to find workNear schools, towns
PaymentSales
Realistic earningsLow to medium
Time to first incomeWeeks

How to start: Stock ahead of January and term openings. Pros: Predictable peaks. Cons & risks: Seasonal. Scam warning: Avoid pirated textbooks. Kenya notes: School openings bring big rushes.

871. Kitchenware and household goods shop

What it is: Selling cookware, plastics, cutlery, flasks and home items. How it works: Buy from wholesalers or importers → retail.

SkillsMerchandising
Cost & equipmentPremises, stock
Where to find workEstates, markets
PaymentSales
Realistic earningsLow to medium
Time to first incomeWeeks

How to start: Display attractively; offer wedding and housewarming bundles. Pros: Wide market. Cons & risks: Competition from online sellers. Scam warning: Check quality before buying stock. Kenya notes: Gikomba and Eastleigh wholesalers supply many shops.

872. Fabric and haberdashery shop

What it is: Selling fabrics, threads, buttons, zips and tailoring supplies. How it works: Stock fabrics and accessories → sell to tailors and customers.

SkillsFabric knowledge
Cost & equipmentPremises, stock
Where to find workNear tailors, markets
PaymentSales
Realistic earningsLow to medium
Time to first incomeWeeks

How to start: Know which fabrics local tailors use (kitenge, uniform fabrics). Pros: Tailors buy regularly. Cons & risks: Fashion trends change. Scam warning: Avoid overstocking fashion fabrics. Kenya notes: Pairs well with tailoring businesses (method 267).

873. Baby shop

What it is: Selling baby clothes, diapers, feeding items and gifts. How it works: Stock essentials → sell to parents and gift buyers.

SkillsProduct knowledge
Cost & equipmentPremises or online shop, stock
Where to find workNear hospitals, estates, online
PaymentSales
Realistic earningsLow to medium
Time to first incomeWeeks

How to start: Start online with essentials; add a physical shop later. Pros: Repeat customers. Cons & risks: Safety standards matter. Scam warning: Sell only safe, standard products (cots, car seats). Kenya notes: Baby showers and hospital visits drive gift sales.

874. Wines and spirits shop (licensed)

What it is: Selling alcoholic drinks legally. How it works: Get a county liquor licence → stock → sell within legal hours.

SkillsRetail management, compliance
Cost & equipmentPremises, licence, stock
Where to find workTowns, estates
PaymentSales
Realistic earningsMedium
Time to first incomeMonths

How to start: Apply for the county liquor licence and follow trading hours. Pros: Good margins. Cons & risks: Strict rules; social responsibility. Scam warning: Never sell to under-18s or sell illicit or counterfeit alcohol. Kenya notes: The Alcoholic Drinks Control Act and county laws regulate licences and hours.

875. Mitumba (second-hand clothes) market stall

What it is: Selling second-hand clothes from a physical stall at a market. How it works: Buy bales → sort grades → price and sell.

SkillsSorting, pricing, selling
Cost & equipmentStall rent, bale capital
Where to find workMarkets, roadside stalls
PaymentDaily sales
Realistic earningsLow to medium
Time to first incomeDays

How to start: Learn grading from experienced traders before buying expensive bales. Pros: Popular with buyers. Cons & risks: Bale quality is a gamble. Scam warning: Buy from reputable bale dealers. Kenya notes: Selling online is method 230; a stall reaches walk-in buyers.

Summary

  • Neighbourhood retail includes dukas, hardware, cereals, milk ATMs, stationery, kitchenware, fabrics, baby shops, licensed wines and spirits, and mitumba stalls.
  • Get the right county and product licences.
  • Control stock and credit, and use honest scales.
