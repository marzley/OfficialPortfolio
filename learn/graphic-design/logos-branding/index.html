Logos and branding: brand strategy, types of logos, the logo design process, brand guidelines and delivering files

A logo is the face of a business: it appears on signboards, receipts, uniforms, social media profiles, vehicles, packaging and websites. But a logo is just one part of a brand: the whole personality and promise a business communicates through its name, colours, fonts, images, tone of voice and customer experience. Logo and branding projects are among the best-paid graphic design jobs, and every new business, church, school, NGO, SACCO, event or product needs one. This unit teaches how to design logos professionally, build complete brand identities, and deliver files clients can actually use.

Brand, identity and logo

TermMeaning
BrandHow people perceive the business (reputation, feelings, promise)
Brand identityThe visible elements: logo, colours, fonts, imagery, patterns, voice
LogoThe main mark that identifies the business

You can't fully design a brand's reputation (that comes from experience), but you can design an identity that communicates the right personality consistently.

Brand strategy: questions to ask

  1. What does the business do, and for whom?
  2. What makes it different from competitors?
  3. Three to five words describing its personality (e.g. modern, friendly, trustworthy, premium, playful).
  4. Who are the main competitors, and how do they look?
  5. Where will the logo be used? (signboard, embroidery on uniforms, small app icon, vehicle branding, stamps)
  6. Any colours, symbols or styles to use or avoid (cultural, religious, competitor colours)?
  7. Budget and timeline.

Types of logos

TypeDescriptionExamples of use

Lettermark/monogramInitialsLong names (e.g. a law firm's initials)
Symbol/iconA picture or abstract markBrands that want recognition without words (needs time and budget to build recognition)
Combination markSymbol + nameThe most flexible choice for small businesses
EmblemText inside a badge or crestSchools, clubs, government, coffee brands
MascotA characterFood brands, sports teams, kids' products

What makes a good logo

  • Simple: recognisable at a glance and at small sizes (a social media profile picture or a stamp).
  • Memorable and distinctive: not generic clip art or a copy of a big brand.
  • Appropriate: fits the industry and audience (a children's school vs a law firm).
  • Versatile: works in colour and one colour, on light and dark backgrounds, printed, embroidered, engraved.
  • Timeless: avoid trendy effects that date quickly (heavy gradients, 3D bevels, drop shadows).
  • Legible: clear fonts; test at 16 px and at billboard size.

The logo design process

  1. Brief and strategy (questions above), agreed in writing.
  2. Research: competitors, industry symbols, mood board (Pinterest board of styles, colours, fonts).
  3. Sketching: 20–50 quick thumbnail sketches on paper. Quantity first; most ideas will be discarded.
  4. Digital drafts: build the best 5–10 in a vector tool (Illustrator, Inkscape, Affinity Designer, Figma). Design in black and white first so the shape works without colour.
  5. Present 2–3 concepts to the client, shown in real-life mock-ups (signboard, business card, phone screen, T-shirt) with a short explanation of the idea behind each.
  6. Refine the chosen concept: spacing, curves, font adjustments, colour.
  7. Finalise and deliver the full logo package and guidelines.

Canva can be used for simple wordmarks and drafts, but professional logos should be built as vectors so they scale perfectly; check Canva's licence terms too: you generally can't trademark logos made from Canva's stock elements and templates.

Colour and typography in logos

  • 1–2 main colours plus neutrals; check meaning and competitors (see the colour lessons).
  • Ensure the logo works in one colour (black, white) for stamps, embroidery and photocopies.
  • Choose fonts with the right personality; customise letters slightly for uniqueness; check the font licence allows commercial logo use.
  • Avoid thin details that disappear when small or embroidered.

Brand guidelines

A short PDF (5–15 pages) that tells everyone how to use the brand:

  • Logo versions, clear space (minimum empty space around the logo), minimum size.
  • Incorrect uses (don't stretch, rotate, recolour, add effects, place on busy backgrounds).
  • Colour palette with HEX (screen), RGB, CMYK (print) and, if needed, Pantone codes.
  • Typography: heading and body fonts, sizes.
  • Imagery style, icon style, patterns.
  • Tone of voice examples.
  • Applications: business card, letterhead, social media templates, signage.

Delivering files

FileUse
AI / EPS / PDF (vector)Printers, signage, merchandise
SVGWebsites
PNG (transparent background)Documents, presentations, social media, websites
JPGQuick previews (no transparency)
Favicon (ICO/PNG 32–512 px)Website tab icon

Provide colour, black and white versions, horizontal and stacked, in organised folders (Print / Web / Social), plus the guidelines PDF. Use RGB files for screens and CMYK for print.

Pricing, contracts and ethics

  • Price by scope: number of concepts, revisions, variations, guidelines, stationery. A simple logo, a logo + mini brand guide, and a full identity package are different products (see the pricing lessons in Make Money Online).
  • Take a deposit (e.g. 50%) before starting; balance before final files.
  • State ownership: usually the client receives full rights to the final logo after payment; you may keep the right to show it in your portfolio.
  • Never copy existing logos or trace other designers' work; search for similar logos before finalising. Clients may want to register a trademark (in Kenya, with KIPI, the Kenya Industrial Property Institute), so originality matters.
Think about it: A new SACCO asks for a logo "with a handshake and a globe, in green and gold, like all other SACCOs". How would you guide them toward a stronger logo?Show answer

Run a brief: their members, values and what makes them different. Show competitor research revealing that handshakes, globes and green/gold are overused, making them hard to remember. Propose distinctive concepts (e.g. a simple symbol tied to their name or community, a strong wordmark), designed in black and white first, presented in real-world mock-ups (cards, app icon, signage), while still respecting trust-related colour preferences.

Summary

  • A brand is perception; the identity (logo, colours, fonts, imagery, voice) communicates it consistently.
  • Ask strategy questions; choose the right logo type (wordmark, lettermark, symbol, combination, emblem, mascot).
  • Good logos are simple, memorable, appropriate, versatile, timeless and legible.
  • Process: brief, research, sketches, vector drafts in black and white, 2–3 concepts in mock-ups, refinement, delivery.
  • Deliver variations and file formats with brand guidelines; price by scope, take deposits, clarify ownership and never copy.
