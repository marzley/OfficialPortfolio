Option 6: Global stocks and ETFs

Many Kenyans now invest in companies like Apple, Microsoft or Safaricom's global peers, or in exchange-traded funds (ETFs) that hold hundreds of companies at once, through international or local investment apps. This lesson explains how it works and what to watch out for.

1. What it is

  • Global stocks: shares of companies listed on foreign exchanges (e.g. the New York Stock Exchange, Nasdaq, London Stock Exchange).
  • ETFs: funds traded on an exchange like a share, which hold a basket of assets. An S&P 500 ETF, for example, holds about 500 large US companies. Others track world markets, specific sectors, bonds or gold.
  • Index funds: funds designed to match a market index at low cost (many ETFs are index funds).
  • Fractional shares: some platforms let you buy a fraction of an expensive share (e.g. $10 worth of a $400 share).

2. How it works: example

You invest $100 a month into a broad world or S&P 500 index ETF through an app.

  • You own a tiny slice of hundreds of companies.
  • Over many years, returns come from company profit growth and dividends; prices also fall significantly at times (e.g. 20–30%+ in bad years).
  • You don't need to pick individual winners.

Versus buying one company: if that company struggles, your whole investment suffers. ETFs spread that risk.

3. How to start

Options available to Kenyans change over time; typically:

  1. International brokers that accept clients from Kenya (check their regulator, e.g. the US SEC/FINRA or the UK FCA, and whether they accept Kenyan residents).
  2. Local platforms or fund managers offering access to global shares or global funds; check they're licensed by the CMA.
  3. Local unit trusts / collective investment schemes that invest offshore, managed by CMA-licensed fund managers.

Steps: verify regulation → open an account (ID, proof of address, tax forms such as the US W-8BEN for non-US persons on US platforms) → fund it (bank wire, card or other methods; compare fees and exchange rates) → buy ETFs or shares → hold and review periodically.

4. Costs

  • Trading commissions (some platforms advertise zero commission but earn elsewhere)
  • Currency conversion from KES to USD and back, often the biggest hidden cost
  • Deposit/withdrawal fees (international transfers)
  • ETF expense ratios (annual fees inside the fund; broad index ETFs are often very low)
  • Dividend withholding tax by the foreign country (e.g. US dividends paid to non-US persons are taxed at source)
  • Possible inactivity or custody fees

5. Risk level: medium (diversified ETFs, long term) to high (single stocks, short-term)

  • Market falls can be large and last for years.
  • Currency risk: if the shilling strengthens against the dollar, your dollar investments are worth fewer shillings (and vice versa).
  • Platform risk: understand how your assets are held (custody) and what investor protection applies in the platform's country.
  • Single stocks and sector bets can fall sharply.

6. Regulation

Your protection depends on where the platform is regulated and which entity holds your account. Kenyan regulation (CMA) applies to locally licensed firms; foreign brokers are regulated in their home countries, and complaints go there. Check carefully, and avoid unregulated "investment apps".

7. Who it may suit

  • Long-term investors who want global diversification, with money they won't need for 5–10+ years.
  • People comfortable with dollar exposure and some paperwork.
  • A popular simple approach: regular monthly investing into a broad, low-cost index ETF ("dollar-cost averaging"), regardless of short-term news.

8. Red flags and scams

  • "Invest in Tesla/Apple through me" (send money to an individual)
  • Apps that aren't regulated anywhere, promising fixed returns from "stock trading"
  • Unverified "AI stock-picking" services with guaranteed results
  • Leveraged CFDs on stocks presented as "owning shares" (check whether you actually own the share)

9. Verdict

Owning global stocks and especially low-cost diversified ETFs is one of the most widely recommended long-term investing approaches internationally. From Kenya, the main challenges are choosing a properly regulated platform, managing currency and transfer costs, and staying patient through market falls. It's investing, not quick income.
