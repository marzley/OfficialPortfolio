Option 8: Options and futures

Options and futures are derivatives traded on regulated exchanges worldwide. Farmers, airlines, banks and fund managers use them to manage risk (hedging); traders use them to speculate. They're very different from the fixed-payout "binary options" sold online. They're also complex; this lesson is an introduction.

1. What they are

Futures

A futures contract is an agreement to buy or sell an asset at a set price on a set future date. Examples: futures on stock indices, oil, gold, maize, currencies and interest rates.

  • Both sides must fulfil the contract (or close it before expiry).
  • Traded with margin (a deposit), so they're leveraged.
  • Profits and losses are settled daily ("marked to market").

Options

An option gives the buyer the right, but not the obligation, to buy (a call) or sell (a put) an asset at a set strike price before or at expiry. The buyer pays a premium to the seller (writer).

  • Option buyers can lose at most the premium they paid.
  • Option sellers receive the premium but can face large (sometimes unlimited) losses.

2. How they work: examples

Futures (hedging): a Kenyan importer expects to pay dollars in three months. To protect against the shilling weakening, they lock in an exchange rate with a futures or forward contract. If the shilling weakens, the contract gain offsets the higher cost of dollars.

Futures (speculation): a trader buys one index futures contract. If the index rises 2%, the contract gains about 2% of its full value, but only a fraction was deposited as margin, so the gain (or loss) on the deposit is much larger.

Call option: a share trades at KSh 100. You buy a 3-month call with strike KSh 110 for a premium of KSh 4.

  • At expiry the share is KSh 125 → the option is worth KSh 15; profit ≈ KSh 11 per share (15 − 4).
  • At expiry the share is KSh 105 → the option expires worthless; you lose the KSh 4 premium.

3. Costs

Exchange and broker fees, bid-ask spreads (wide on less-traded contracts), margin financing, and for options, time decay: an option loses value as expiry approaches if price doesn't move enough.

4. Risk level: high (and complex)

  • Leverage in futures magnifies losses; margin calls can require extra deposits quickly.
  • Options pricing depends on volatility, time and interest rates; beginners often buy cheap options that expire worthless.
  • Selling options can create large losses.
  • Liquidity can be thin in smaller markets.

5. In Kenya

The NSE runs a derivatives market (NEXT), regulated by the CMA, which has offered futures contracts such as index futures and single-stock futures. Trading requires a licensed derivatives broker and margin. Check the NSE's current product list, contract specifications and participating brokers. Access to large international options and futures markets (US, Europe) is possible through international brokers that accept Kenyan clients; check regulation carefully.

6. Who it may suit

  • Businesses and professionals hedging real risks (importers, exporters, farmers, fund managers).
  • Experienced investors who understand pricing, margin and risk thoroughly.
  • Not suitable for beginners looking for quick profits.

7. How to learn safely

  1. Master basic investing and risk management first.
  2. Study options and futures from exchange education resources (major exchanges publish free courses) and reputable textbooks.
  3. Paper-trade (simulate) for months.
  4. If trading, start with defined-risk strategies (e.g. buying options with small premiums, or spreads) and tiny sizes.

8. Red flags

  • Anyone calling short-expiry binary bets "options trading"
  • Promises of "weekly income selling options" with no explanation of risk
  • Unregulated platforms offering "futures" with extreme leverage

9. Verdict

Exchange-traded options and futures are powerful, legitimate tools mainly designed for risk management, and they can be used for speculation by skilled traders. They're complex and leveraged; most beginners should understand them as part of financial education rather than trade them.
