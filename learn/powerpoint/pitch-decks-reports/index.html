Pitch decks, reports and training slides: structures that persuade and teach

Different presentations have different jobs. A pitch deck persuades investors, clients or a grant panel; a report presentation informs managers or a board; a training deck teaches skills; a proposal wins a contract. Each has a proven structure. This unit gives you templates for the most common types, with slide-by-slide guidance and Kenyan examples, so you never start from a blank slide again.

What you will learn

  • The investor/business pitch deck (10–12 slides)
  • Client proposals and sales presentations
  • Board and progress reports
  • Training and teaching presentations
  • School and university project defences
  • Storytelling techniques that make presentations memorable

The business pitch deck

Used for investors, accelerators, competitions, grant panels and partners. Keep it to about 10–12 slides and aim to speak for 5–10 minutes.

#SlideWhat to include
1TitleCompany name, logo, one-line description ("Affordable solar-powered cold storage for smallholder farmers"), your name and contacts
2ProblemThe pain, who has it and why it matters (with a number or story: "30–40% of tomatoes spoil before reaching market" type evidence, cited)
3SolutionYour product/service and how it solves the problem; a photo or demo screenshot
4MarketWho your customers are and how many (target market size, realistically estimated)
5Product / how it worksKey features, simple diagram, demo
6Business modelHow you make money (pricing, subscriptions, commissions), unit economics if known
7TractionProof: customers, revenue, growth, pilots, partnerships, testimonials
8CompetitionAlternatives (including "doing nothing") and your advantage
9Go-to-marketHow you'll reach customers (channels, partnerships, sales strategy)
10TeamFounders and key people, relevant experience
11FinancialsRevenue so far, projections (realistic), key assumptions
12The askWhat you want (amount, what it will be used for, milestones it unlocks), and contacts

Tips:

  • Lead with the problem: people fund solutions to problems they understand.
  • Show evidence, not just claims: numbers, photos, pilots, letters of support.
  • One idea per slide with a clear headline ("We've grown 25% month-on-month since March").
  • Be honest about risks and assumptions; investors respect realism.
  • Prepare a short version (3 minutes) and a detailed appendix for questions.

Client proposal / sales presentation

SlideContent
Their situationShow you understand the client's business and goals
The problem/opportunityWhat's holding them back or what they could gain
Your solutionWhat you'll deliver (e.g. website + M-Pesa payments + SEO)
How it works / processSteps and timeline
ProofSimilar projects, case studies, testimonials
InvestmentPackages and prices (clear, with what's included)
Next stepsHow to start (sign, deposit, kickoff date)

Tailor every proposal: use the client's name, logo colours (tastefully) and their own words from your discussion.

Board, management and progress reports

Busy leaders want the conclusion first:

  1. Summary slide: key results and decisions needed (e.g. "Q3 sales up 12%; approve KSh 2M marketing budget").
  2. Key metrics (a small dashboard: sales, costs, customers, targets vs actual, with traffic-light status).
  3. What went well / challenges with causes.
  4. Actions and recommendations.
  5. Decisions needed (clearly listed).
  6. Appendix: detailed tables for reference.

Use consistent charts across reporting periods so trends are easy to compare.

Training and teaching presentations

Learning needs structure, practice and checks:

PartContent
Learning objectives"By the end, you will be able to..." (2–4 specific outcomes)
Why it mattersRelevance to learners' work/lives
Content in small chunksOne concept per slide; examples; visuals; demonstrations
ActivitiesPractice tasks, discussions, quizzes every 10–15 minutes
Check understandingQuestions, polls (Mentimeter, Slido, Google Forms), short quizzes
SummaryKey points recap
Next steps/resourcesHandouts, links, assignments

For TVET and CBET trainers, align slides with competency units and performance criteria, and include practical demonstrations and assessment checkpoints.

Conclusions and recommendations

Practise answering likely panel questions: why this method, how you validated results, what you'd improve, and your own contribution.

Storytelling techniques

  • Start with a person: "Meet Mama Wanjiru, who loses a third of her mangoes every season..."
  • Problem → struggle → solution → result arc.
  • Contrast: before vs after (time saved, money earned).
  • Concrete numbers: "saves 3 hours a week" beats "saves time".
  • Rule of three: three key points are memorable.
  • End with a clear call to action.
Think about it: A startup's first slide is a detailed technical architecture diagram of their app. Why might investors lose interest, and what should come first?Show answer

Investors first need to understand why it matters: who has the problem and how big it is. A technical diagram before the problem is confusing and doesn't show value. Start with the problem and solution in plain language; keep technical details for an appendix or Q&A.

Practice tasks

  1. Create a 10-slide pitch deck for a real or imagined local business.
  2. Turn a recent report or assignment into a 6-slide management presentation with the conclusion first.
  3. Build a 15-minute training deck with objectives, two activities and a quiz.
  4. Write the opening story for your pitch in three sentences.
  5. Rehearse your pitch in 3 minutes and in 10 minutes.

Summary

  • Pitch decks: problem, solution, market, product, business model, traction, competition, go-to-market, team, financials, ask.
  • Proposals: their situation, problem, your solution, process, proof, investment, next steps.
  • Reports: conclusion and decisions first, consistent metrics, appendix for details.
  • Training: objectives, chunks, activities, checks, summary.
  • Defences: problem, objectives, methodology, results, conclusions, limitations.
  • Storytelling: people, contrast, concrete numbers, rule of three, clear call to action.
