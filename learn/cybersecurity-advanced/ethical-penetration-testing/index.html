Ethical penetration testing: permission, scope, methodology, legal practice labs, bug bounties and report writing

A penetration test is an authorised, simulated attack that shows an organisation how a real attacker could get in, so they can fix it first. The difference between a pentester and a criminal is permission, a defined scope, and a report that helps the client.

Types of security testing

Penetration testConfirm weaknesses can be exploited within agreed limits, show the impact
Red team exerciseLong, stealthy, goal-based simulation testing people, process and technology, including detection
Purple teamAttackers and defenders work together to improve detections

Step 1: pre-engagement (never skip this)

Before touching anything, get in writing:

  • Authorisation signed by someone who actually has the authority to approve testing of those systems (the system owner, not a junior staff member). Cloud and hosting providers may have their own testing policies too.
  • Scope: exact domains, IP ranges, apps and accounts in scope, and what is out of scope.
  • Rules of engagement: testing dates and times, allowed techniques (is social engineering allowed? denial of service is usually forbidden), how to handle sensitive data found, emergency contacts, and what happens if you find signs of a real attacker.
  • A contract or statement of work covering confidentiality and liability.

Step 2 onwards: the methodology

Recognised frameworks include the PTES (Penetration Testing Execution Standard), the OWASP Web Security Testing Guide and NIST SP 800-115.

PhaseGoalTypical tools (authorised use only)
ReconnaissanceLearn about the target from public sourcesSearch engines, DNS records, certificate transparency logs
Scanning and enumerationFind hosts, open ports, services and versionsNmap, web directory discovery tools
Vulnerability analysisMatch findings to known weaknessesVulnerability scanners, manual review
ExploitationProve a weakness is real, carefully and within scopeFrameworks such as Metasploit, Burp Suite
Post-exploitationShow business impact (only as far as the rules allow)Evidence collection, documenting access
ReportingExplain findings and fixes clearlyReport templates, screenshots
Clean-upRemove test accounts, files and tools you createdYour testing notes

Stay inside the lines

Stop and contact the client immediately if you could cause damage, find evidence of a real breach, or touch highly sensitive personal data. Never copy more data than needed to prove a finding, and never keep client data after the engagement.

Writing the report

The report is the product the client pays for. Include:

  • Executive summary: plain language for managers: overall risk, top issues, key recommendations.
  • Scope and methodology: what was tested, when and how.
  • Findings: for each one: title, severity (e.g. CVSS), affected systems, description, evidence (screenshots), business impact, and step-by-step remediation.
  • Positive findings: good controls you noticed.
  • Appendices: tools used, detailed output.

Example finding title: "High: Admin login page accessible from the internet without MFA, allowing password-guessing attacks. Fix: restrict access by IP or VPN and enforce MFA."

Bug bounties

Platforms such as HackerOne, Bugcrowd and Intigriti host programmes where companies invite testing.

  • Read each programme's policy: scope, banned techniques, safe-harbour terms.
  • Test only listed assets, use your own test accounts, never access other users' data.
  • Expect duplicates and rejections at first; most beginners learn on labs for months before their first valid bug.
  • A programme's permission covers only what its policy says; a company without a programme has not invited testing.

In Kenya

Kenyan companies are increasingly running vulnerability disclosure and bug bounty programmes, and local CTF competitions and security communities (meetups, university clubs, Africa-focused cybersecurity conferences) are great places to learn and network. Check each event's rules and only test what organisers provide.

Think about it: During an authorised web test, you find a database backup file on the server containing thousands of customers' ID numbers and phone numbers. What should you do?Show answer

Stop, don't download or open more than is needed to confirm what it is, and notify the client's emergency contact immediately as the rules of engagement require. Document the finding with minimal evidence (e.g. the file name and a redacted sample), recommend removing it and checking access logs to see if anyone else downloaded it, and don't keep any copy of the data.

Summary

  • Permission, scope and rules of engagement come before any testing.
  • Methodology: reconnaissance, scanning, vulnerability analysis, exploitation, post-exploitation, reporting, clean-up.
  • The report, with clear impact and fixes, is the real deliverable.
  • Practise only on legal platforms and your own lab; follow bug bounty policies exactly.
