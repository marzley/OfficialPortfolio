Parts of a computer: input, output, processing and storage devices explained

Whether you're buying a laptop, fixing a desktop in a school lab, sitting a computer studies exam or explaining a problem to a technician, you need to know the parts of a computer and what each one does. This unit explains every main part in plain language, how they work together, how to read a laptop's specifications, and what matters when buying a computer in Kenya.

The four jobs of every computer

Every computer, from a phone to a supercomputer, does four things:

JobMeaningExample: typing a letter
InputGetting data inYou press keys on the keyboard
ProcessingWorking on the dataThe CPU works out which letter to show
StorageKeeping dataThe document is saved on the SSD
OutputShowing resultsThe letter appears on screen, or prints

Hardware and software

Hardware is the physical parts you can touch (keyboard, screen, CPU). Software is the programs and instructions that run on the hardware (Windows, Word, Chrome). A computer needs both: hardware without software does nothing; software can't run without hardware.

Inside the system unit

The system unit (a desktop's box or the body of a laptop) holds the main components.

CPU (processor): the brain

The Central Processing Unit carries out instructions: calculations, decisions, moving data.

  • Makers: Intel (Core i3, i5, i7, i9, Core Ultra; Celeron/Pentium for budget) and AMD (Ryzen 3, 5, 7, 9). Apple uses its own M-series chips.
  • Cores: more cores handle more tasks at once (a quad-core CPU has 4).
  • Speed (GHz): cycles per second; higher is faster within the same generation.
  • Generation matters: a recent Core i5 can beat an old Core i7. Look for the generation number (e.g. "Intel Core i5-1235U" is 12th generation).

RAM (memory): the work desk

Random Access Memory holds programs and files you're currently using, for fast access. It's temporary: it empties when the computer turns off.

Think of RAM as your desk and storage as your cupboard: a bigger desk lets you work on more things at once without fetching from the cupboard.

RAMGood for
4 GBVery basic use; slow with Windows 11 and many browser tabs
8 GBOffice work, browsing, online classes (a sensible minimum today)
16 GBProgramming, design, video editing, heavy multitasking
32 GB+Professional video, 3D, large data

Storage: the cupboard

Storage keeps files and programs permanently (even when off).

TypeSpeedNotes
HDD (hard disk drive)SlowSpinning disks; cheap per GB; fragile if dropped
SSD (solid state drive, SATA)FastNo moving parts; much faster boot and loading
NVMe SSDFastestPlugs directly into the motherboard (M.2 slot)

Upgrading an old laptop from an HDD to an SSD is often the single best speed improvement (covered in the computer maintenance subject).

Motherboard

The main circuit board that connects everything: CPU, RAM, storage, ports and power. Other parts plug into it.

Graphics (GPU)

Handles images and video. Integrated graphics (built into the CPU) are fine for office work and video; a dedicated graphics card (NVIDIA GeForce, AMD Radeon) is needed for gaming, 3D, heavy video editing and some AI work.

Power supply and battery

Desktops have a PSU (power supply unit). Laptops have a battery and a charger (adapter). Use the correct charger; cheap fake chargers can damage laptops.

Cooling

Fans and heat sinks remove heat. Dust blocks them, causing overheating and slowdowns, especially in dusty environments; cleaning vents helps.

Buying tips in Kenya

  • Refurbished/ex-UK business laptops (HP EliteBook/ProBook, Dell Latitude, Lenovo ThinkPad) are popular, durable and affordable. Buy from reputable shops that give a warranty and a receipt.
  • Test before paying: keyboard (every key), screen (dead pixels), battery health, charging, Wi-Fi, webcam, ports, speakers.
  • Insist on an SSD; avoid laptops with only 4 GB RAM and an HDD if you can.
  • Check the battery (power cuts happen) and ask whether it's original.
  • Confirm Windows is genuine and activated.
Think about it: A student has KSh 35,000 for a laptop for university and coding practice. One option is a new laptop with a Celeron CPU, 4 GB RAM and a 500 GB HDD; another is a refurbished Core i5 (10th gen) with 8 GB RAM and a 256 GB SSD. Which is better and why?Show answer

The refurbished Core i5 with 8 GB RAM and an SSD will be far faster and more useful for coding and multitasking. The new Celeron with 4 GB RAM and an HDD would be slow with modern software. "New" isn't always better: specifications matter more, as long as the refurbished laptop comes from a reputable seller with a warranty.

Looking after your hardware

  • Use a surge protector or UPS for desktops (power fluctuations damage components).
  • Keep laptops on hard, flat surfaces (beds and pillows block vents).
  • Keep drinks away; clean dust from vents.
  • Shut down properly; don't pull the power during updates.
  • Carry laptops in a padded bag; don't lift them by the screen.
  • Back up important files (cloud or external drive): hardware can fail.

Summary

  • Computers do input → processing → output, with storage keeping data.
  • Input devices: keyboard, mouse, touchscreen, microphone, camera, scanner. Output: monitor, printer, speakers, projector.
  • Inside: CPU (brain), RAM (temporary work memory), storage (HDD/SSD, permanent), motherboard, GPU, power and cooling.
  • Ports: USB-A, USB-C, HDMI, VGA, Ethernet, audio.
  • For most people today: a recent i5/Ryzen 5, 8 GB+ RAM and an SSD; buy refurbished from reputable sellers with a warranty and test everything.
